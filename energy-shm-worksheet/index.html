1. Socratic Derivation: v = ±ω√(x₀² − x²)

We shall derive the velocity–displacement relation for a particle executing SHM using the principle of conservation of mechanical energy. Consider a mass–spring system with spring constant k and mass m, oscillating with amplitude x0.

Step 1 ▶ Write the total mechanical energy of the system at an arbitrary displacement x where the particle has velocity v. Etotal = ½mv² + ½kx² The total energy is the sum of kinetic energy (½mv²) and elastic potential energy (½kx²).
Step 2 ▶ What is the velocity at the maximum displacement x = x0? Hence write Etotal in terms of k and x0 only. At x = x0, v = 0  ⇒  Etotal = ½kx0² At the turning points the particle is momentarily at rest; all energy is stored as potential energy in the spring.
Step 3 ▶ Energy is conserved. Equate the two expressions for Etotal. ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kx0² No energy enters or leaves the system — mechanical energy is strictly conserved in ideal SHM.
Step 4 ▶ Multiply through by 2 and rearrange to isolate the term containing v². mv² = kx0² − kx² = k(x0² − x²) The kinetic energy term is now isolated. Divide by m to obtain v².
Step 5 ▶ Divide by m to obtain an expression for v². v² = (k/m)(x0² − x²) The ratio k/m governs the stiffness-to-inertia balance of the oscillator.
Step 6 ▶ Recall the defining relation for angular frequency in SHM: ω² = k/m. Substitute and take the square root. v² = ω²(x0² − x²)  ⇒  v = ±ω√(x0² − x²) The ± sign indicates that the particle may be travelling in either direction (towards or away from equilibrium) at a given displacement.
v = ±ω√(x0² − x²)

2. Definition and Conditions for Simple Harmonic Motion

Simple harmonic motion is defined as an oscillation in which the acceleration a is directly proportional to the displacement x from a fixed point and is always directed towards that point.

a = −ω²x

where ω is the angular frequency (measured in rad s−1). The negative sign indicates that the acceleration is always directed towards the equilibrium position (restoring). Equivalently:

  • Restoring force: F = −kx  (where k = mω² is the force constant)
  • Condition for SHM: a ∝ −x
  • Angular frequency: ω = 2πf = 2π/T

For a body to execute SHM, the restoring force must obey Hooke's law (F = −kx) over the range of motion.

3. Kinematics of Simple Harmonic Motion

The displacement of a particle executing SHM can be described by a sinusoidal function of time:

x = x0 sin(ωt)   or   x = x0 cos(ωt)

Differentiating with respect to time gives expressions for velocity and acceleration:

4. Period of Oscillation for Common SHM Systems

4.1 Mass–Spring System

The period of a mass–spring oscillator depends only on the mass m and the spring constant k. Let us derive T = 2π√(m/k) step by step.

Step 1 ▶ Write the restoring force on a mass m attached to a spring of force constant k at displacement x. F = −kx Hooke's law: the spring exerts a restoring force proportional to displacement and directed towards equilibrium.
Step 2 ▶ Apply Newton's second law: F = ma. Substitute the restoring force. ma = −kx  ⇒  a = −(k/m)x The acceleration is proportional to −x with constant of proportionality (k/m).
Step 3 ▶ Recall the defining equation for SHM: a = −ω²x. Compare with Step 2 to identify ω. ω² = k/m  ⇒  ω = √(k/m) The angular frequency is determined by the stiffness-to-mass ratio. A stiffer spring (larger k) or a lighter mass gives a higher frequency.
Step 4 ▶ Use the relation between angular frequency and period: ω = 2π/T. Substitute for ω. 2π/T = √(k/m)  ⇒  T = 2π√(m/k) Conceptual insight: Larger mass → more inertia → slower oscillation. Larger k → stiffer spring → faster oscillation. The period does NOT depend on amplitude — this is the property of isochronism.
T = 2π√(m/k)    Mass–spring period — independent of g and amplitude

4.2 Simple Pendulum (small-angle approximation)

For a pendulum bob of mass m on a string of length L, the restoring force is gravity. Let us derive T = 2π√(L/g).

Step 1 ▶ Consider the pendulum displaced by a small angle θ from equilibrium. Resolve the weight mg into components. What is the restoring force? Restoring force = −mg sinθ   (the component perpendicular to the string) The tension in the string balances mg cosθ. The net restoring force comes from the tangential component of weight.
Step 2 ▶ For small angles, sinθ ≈ θ (in radians). Also relate the arc displacement x to θ: x = Lθ. F ≈ −mgθ = −mg(x/L) = −(mg/L)x Critical approximation: For θ < 10° (0.17 rad), sinθ differs from θ by less than 0.5%. Without this approximation, the motion would not be simple harmonic.
Step 3 ▶ Apply Newton's second law: F = ma. Use the expression for F from Step 2. ma = −(mg/L)x  ⇒  a = −(g/L)x Comparing with a = −ω²x, we see that (g/L) plays the role of ω². The mass m cancels out!
Step 4 ▶ Identify ω and then derive T using ω = 2π/T. ω² = g/L  ⇒  ω = √(g/L)  ⇒  T = 2π√(L/g) Conceptual insights: The period is independent of mass (m cancels). Longer pendulum → larger period. Weaker gravity (smaller g) → larger period. This formula enables accurate measurement of g by timing a pendulum.
T = 2π√(L/g)    Pendulum period — independent of mass and amplitude (small angles only)

Key Observations from the Graphs

  • Energy conservation: The sum Ek + Ep is constant at every displacement — the horizontal line in Fig. 1.
  • Phase relationship: When Ek is maximum (at equilibrium, x = 0), Ep is zero, and vice versa at the turning points.
  • Frequency doubling: Both Ek and Ep complete two cycles per oscillation period because each passes through a maximum twice per cycle.
  • Velocity–displacement ellipse: The area enclosed by the ellipse in the v–x plane is proportional to the total energy of the system.
  • Damping: In a real system, energy is dissipated to the surroundings (e.g. air resistance, internal friction), causing the amplitude and total energy to decrease over time.

6. Damping, Forced Oscillations and Resonance

3.1 Light, Critical and Heavy Damping

The degree of damping determines how quickly the oscillation amplitude decays:

  • Light damping: Amplitude decays exponentially over many oscillations. The system continues to oscillate but with decreasing energy.
  • Critical damping: The system returns to equilibrium in the shortest possible time without oscillating. Used in car suspension systems and galvanometer design.
  • Heavy (over)damping: The system returns to equilibrium slowly without oscillating.

3.2 Resonance and Energy Transfer

When a driving frequency matches the natural frequency of the system, resonance occurs. At resonance:

  • The amplitude of oscillation reaches a maximum.
  • Energy transfer from the driver to the oscillator is most efficient.
  • The width of the resonance curve depends on the degree of damping: lighter damping gives a sharper, taller peak.
  • At resonance, the driving force is in phase with the velocity, so work done by the driving force is always positive — energy is fed into the system each cycle.

  • As damping increases, the resonance peak amplitude decreases.
  • The resonance curve becomes broader (less sharp).
  • The resonance frequency shifts slightly lower.
  • For very heavy damping, the resonance peak disappears entirely (the system is overdamped).

7. Worked Example

Problem: A 200 g mass oscillates on a spring of spring constant 50 N m−1 with amplitude 4.0 cm. Determine:

(a) the total mechanical energy of the system;
(b) the speed of the mass when the displacement is 2.0 cm from equilibrium;
(c) the kinetic energy and potential energy at this displacement.

Solution:

(a) At maximum displacement, all energy is potential:

Etotal = ½kx0² = ½ × 50 × (0.040)² = ½ × 50 × 1.6 × 10−3

Etotal = 4.0 × 10−2 J = 40 mJ

(b) Use v = ±ω√(x0² − x²). First find ω:

ω = √(k/m) = √(50 / 0.200) = √250 = 15.81 rad s−1

At x = 0.020 m:

v = 15.81 × √(0.040² − 0.020²) = 15.81 × √(1.2 × 10−3)

v = 15.81 × 0.03464 = 0.548 m s−1

(c) At x = 0.020 m:

Ep = ½kx² = ½ × 50 × (0.020)² = 1.0 × 10−2 J = 10 mJ

Ek = Etotal − Ep = 40 − 10 = 30 mJ

(Check: Ek = ½mv² = ½ × 0.200 × 0.548² = 0.0300 J = 30 mJ ✓)

Case Study A — Suspension Bridge Oscillations [6]

A suspension bridge has a natural frequency of oscillation of 0.25 Hz. On a windy day, gusts of wind produce a periodic driving force on the bridge deck at frequencies between 0.10 Hz and 0.50 Hz.

Mark Scheme — Case Study A

(a) B1: Resonance occurs when the driving frequency equals (or is close to) the natural frequency of the bridge. [1]

A1: The driving force is in phase with the velocity of the bridge, so work done by the force is positive throughout each cycle — energy is continuously fed into the system.
